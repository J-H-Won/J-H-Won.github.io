Threshold Robustness Improvement of Ground Fusion

This project analyzed Ground-Fusion, a low-cost mutli-sensor SLAM system for ground robots, and found that three of its safety mechanisms actually caused catastrophic localization failures due to fixed, brittle thresholds. The project fixed these issues by replacing a rigid feature-rejection threshold with a population-adaptive one, swapping a rigid ground-plane constraint for a Cauchy robust loss that gracefully handles non-flat terrain, and replacing a binary wheel-slip gate with continuous covariance inflation that self-recovers from anomalies. Implemented in the Ceres Solver optimization backend and validated on real SLAM benchmarks, these fixes achieved up to 3.9x better localization accuracy in dynamic scenes and recovery from 23x drift cascades, with no performance loss on normal conditions.

Autonomous Warehouse Robotics Platform

This project focuses on developing a scaled-down autonomous warehouse robotics system that mimics real-world logistics environments. The system integrates a fleet of autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) designed to transport payloads across a structured warehouse floor, along with a stationary robotic arm responsible for picking up and transferring items from the delivery robots. A centralized mission hub coordinates robot tasks, manages real-time communication, and oversees traffic flow to ensure smooth operations. The robots are equipped with onboard sensors and inter-robot awareness mechanisms to enable collision avoidance, mission execution, and safe interaction with the robotic arm, creating a functional testbed for studying multi-robot coordination, fleet management, and warehouse automation.

Optimized and Standardized Camera Calibration for USV Navigation

This project engineered a robust, scalable camera calibration process to support autonomous navigation in uncrewed surface vessels by addressing a critical bottleneck in vision-based perception. Traditional calibration methods produced high reprojection errors (±20 pixels), inconsistent results, and slow throughput, limiting reliability at sea. By applying optical principles to define calibration distance and focus, introducing a Laplacian variance–based focus metric, and developing tools such as a live tag-detection feedback system and a web-based interface, the project reduced reprojection error to sub-pixel accuracy (less than1 pixel RMS), streamlined workflows, and standardized results across multiple camera models. These innovations enable engineers to reliably calibrate wide field-of-view fisheye cameras, improve image clarity for machine learning models, and achieve precise geolocation of maritime objects at ranges up to 5 km.

Adaptive Cruise Control w/ Smart Braking

This project focuses on the design, modeling, and simulation of an intelligent vehicle system that automatically maintains safe distances and stable speeds under varying traffic conditions. Using MATLAB and Simulink, the team developed an Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC) model that integrates Proportional-Integral- Derivative (PID) control strategies to dynamically adjust a vehicle’s throttle and braking. The project begins with modeling the vehicle’s mechanical dynamics and deriving a transfer function to inform controller design. A control approach was implemented to minimize overshoot, improve stability, and ensure smooth acceleration and braking responses. Through simulated scenarios involving a “lead” and “ego” car, the system demonstrated robust adaptability, maintaining safe following distances even during sudden deceleration events. The results confirmed the ACC system’s capability to enhance driving safety, comfort, and efficiency. Suggested future improvements include integrating LiDAR and radar sensors with machine learning for model predictive decision-making and advance the system toward higher levels of vehicle autonomy.
